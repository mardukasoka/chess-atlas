Before the Curtain

Axel was fifty when the future stopped behaving like a distant country.

Nothing announced the change. People still caught trams, argued over rent, watched storms form on screens, planted gardens, went to work and came home. Machines became more capable between one ordinary morning and the next.

Axel had spent enough of his life around movements to distrust anyone who claimed history had chosen a side. He wanted humanity to reach the stars. He wanted Earth alive when it did.

Those desires were beginning to sound incompatible.

At demonstrations he watched the edges rather than the stage. At meetings he listened for the assumptions nobody questioned. Systems that were supposed to be separate seemed to react to one another faster than the people inside them understood.

One afternoon an argument about technology became an argument about ownership, then sovereignty, then what counted as human choice. Elsewhere legislators argued over systems they could neither halt nor adequately describe. On a tram, passengers continued home through a city whose infrastructure was quietly learning new habits.

Later, on a rooftop, Axel interrupted something that might have become sabotage, or retaliation, or the first act in a story nobody would later agree had happened.

He left before anyone could decide whose side he was on.

At a community meeting that evening the adults produced increasingly elaborate names for what they thought was happening.

A child supplied a simpler one.

“The Curtain.”

The name remained.
